Three ways to break a system

2026 · Ongoing

Benchmarks tell you a model works. They rarely tell you when it stops working. These three studies each take a system that reports good numbers and move it somewhere it wasn't trained for — a new climate, a poisoned context window, a rubric it can't actually discriminate on.

Agentic evaluation 5,000-question dataset in progress

Can an LLM be trusted to grade another LLM?

A multi-agent pipeline where local models generate, fact-check, score and synthesise answers — built to produce a large judged dataset, and which spent most of its life exposing the ways an LLM judge quietly lies to you.

Two independent models answer the same question. A third fact-checks. A fourth scores both answers against an 8-criterion rubric. A fifth synthesises the winner into a final answer. Everything runs locally through Ollama and CrewAI — no API, no data leaving the machine.

Each answer then gets a six-signal confidence score — judge rubric, semantic similarity, cross-model agreement via BERTScore, verifier/judge concordance, and two independent external fact-checks against Wikipedia and Wikidata. Below 0.68 the answer is regenerated. Missing signals are dropped and the remaining weights renormalised, never silently scored as zero.

01

The judge wasn't discriminating at all

CompassJudger-1 returned identical scores across all 8 rubric criteria — a single global impression wearing eight labels. Every metric downstream looked healthy. Caught it, abandoned the model, and rebuilt on qwen3.5:9b.

02

Position, not quality, was picking the winner

With answers always presented in a fixed order, win rates skewed 60–72% toward one slot. Randomising presentation order pulled it to 43 / 57 — most of the original "preference" was positional bias.

03

One confidence signal was quietly circular

Verifier↔judge agreement correlated 0.737 with the judge score — because it is derived from it. A weight optimiser was gaming an internal restatement instead of finding real corroboration. Excluded it and re-ran.

04

Proving the threshold does real work

Retrying only failed answers can't validate a cutoff — it's selection bias. So 8% of passing answers get retried too, as a control. Sub-threshold answers improve on retry 66.7% of the time (n=144) against 54.7% for controls (n=53): a real gap, and honestly still under-powered.

Adversarial retrieval 2026

What a RAG system does when the retrieved context is wrong

Retrieval-augmented generation is usually evaluated with context that happens to be correct. This study evaluates the other cases on purpose.

The same question set is run against three deliberately different context conditions — clean, padded with irrelevant chunks, and seeded with misleading or partially wrong passages — to find out whether grounding actually protects the answer or just makes a wrong one sound more confident.

Four things are measured across every condition: answer correctness, hallucination rate, retrieval precision and latency. The interesting cell is the third condition, where a well-behaved system should decline rather than confidently repeat the poisoned passage back.
